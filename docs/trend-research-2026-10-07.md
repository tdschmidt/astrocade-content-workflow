## Selection and hook implications

1. Nominate a **specific visible episode**, not just a genre: the viewer should immediately understand what is odd, risky, or debatable. Require a capture goal and rejection condition before spending time on play.
2. Keep trend evidence as a tie-breaker among good episodes. A weak scene cannot be rescued by a topical label. Conversely, a strong original scene does not need to pretend to be timely.
3. Diversify actual mechanics after inspection. Three differently skinned runners are one coverage family. A topical theme may justify one slot; it should not consume the batch.
4. Test a short event cut, a setup-to-payoff cut, and where useful a longer escalation. Choose boundaries from the evidence rather than targeting a universal duration. A small pilot should reveal whether native timing, model latency, or unclear game state limits the controller.
5. Use the user's hook chat as voice direction: a concrete game absurdity or credible immediate reaction. First-person lines must describe this visible attempt, not invented hours, retries, or emotional history. “who gave the group chat a game engine” works best when the actual game supplies the absurdity; it is not a reusable label for all ten games.
6. Generate meaningfully different premises, then critique each against the exact cut. Prefer a specific punchline over generic “watch this,” “satisfying,” or a question that could sit on any gameplay. Do not flatten a sound joke into a literal action description merely because the joke is subjective.
7. Controversy can come from a questionable in-game choice, absurd fictional premise, or recognizable satire. No current political/celebrity scandal was verified in this pass; do not manufacture allegations or bolt scandal names onto unrelated footage.

These are editorial hypotheses to compare with actual captures and later audience results. They are not an algorithmic guarantee of reach.
